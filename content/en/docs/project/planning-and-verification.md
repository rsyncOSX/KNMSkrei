## Establish the baseline

Create an equipment register with a unique project identifier for each maintainable item. Photograph its location and identification plate and link it to system drawings and historical records.

Record:

- Manufacturer, model, serial number, build year and installation date, where known.
- Technical ratings, configuration, modifications and associated equipment.
- Date last operated, total operating hours and hours since the last major overhaul.
- Previous damage, breakdowns, repairs, inspections and outstanding defects.
- Storage conditions, preservation work and the age and type of fuel, oil and coolant remaining in the equipment.
- Current condition, access limitations, missing parts and immediate safeguarding needs.
- Manuals, spare-parts lists, drawings, certificates and available maintenance records.

Distinguish documented facts, personal recollections, assumptions and unknowns. Cite the source and date for each significant historical claim. Missing history should remain visible as an uncertainty rather than being filled in with an estimate presented as fact.

## Register and check the Deltic references

Add the [T18-37K course notes]({{< relref "/docs/T18_37K/T1837K.md" >}}) and their linked source scans to the document register. Record the December 1967 source revision, engine numbers, modification markings and the handbook or bulletin needed to confirm applicability. Retain the PDF filename and page number with measurements, inspection criteria and proposed procedures so another reviewer can trace the source.

[Chapter 13]({{< relref "/docs/T18_37K/T1837K.md" >}}#chapter-13) distinguishes continuous operation from time-limited maximum power and separates typical readings from operating limits. Preserve those distinctions when preparing a trial sheet. Record source-unit discrepancies and transcription notes as questions for specialist review before selecting acceptance values.

[Chapter 15]({{< relref "/docs/T18_37K/T1837K.md" >}}#chapter-15) helps identify unit-removal, replacement, phasing and timing dependencies. Use it to plan special tools, lifting access and inspection points; confirm the complete procedure and installed configuration before work.

## Build the project plan

Use the baseline to create a complete plan, updating it as inspections reveal more information. Each work package should identify its scope, responsible person, dependencies, materials, specialist support, safety arrangements, deliverables and acceptance criteria.

Prioritise securing the vessel, preventing deterioration and resolving safety-critical defects. Coordinate docking, tank access, lifting, electrical isolation and shared machinery-space access with the wider restoration project.

For every work package, estimate optimistic, realistic and pessimistic labour hours, and state the assumptions behind them. Track elapsed duration separately: volunteer availability, procurement, specialist visits and workshop lead times can govern the schedule even when hands-on work is short.

Define team size, skills, work-session frequency and supervision. Compare required hours with actual available capacity, including preparation, documentation, cleanup and rework. Do not publish a completion date until the baseline supports a credible estimate.

## Specialists, tools and parts

- Identify Deltic expertise and competent support for gearboxes, shaft alignment, propellers, electrical installations, pressure vessels and fire systems as required.
- Inventory general tools, lifting and handling equipment, calibrated measuring instruments and engine-specific special tools.
- Obtain the correct maintenance manuals, parts catalogues and drawings for the installed variants.
- Build a parts list with part numbers, quantities, condition, suppliers, lead times and approved alternatives. Verify compatibility before purchasing or fitting substitutes.
- Record which work requires professional inspection, certification or approval and arrange access before closing up equipment.

## Manage unexpected findings

Maintain a risk register for credible events such as hidden corrosion, seized machinery, missing parts, contaminated systems, obsolete components, failed tests and unavailable specialists. Plan contingency time and cost for significant uncertainties.

It is not possible to predict every anomaly. Use a consistent process for new findings: make the equipment safe, record the evidence, assess consequences, obtain a technical decision, revise the work package and repeat affected inspections or tests. Define who may approve changes and who can stop work.

## Verification and commissioning stages

Agree measurable acceptance criteria with the relevant specialists before work begins. Use manufacturer limits and applicable requirements; do not invent generic limits for historic machinery.

| Stage | Required evidence before proceeding |
| --- | --- |
| Baseline review | Equipment register, history, condition survey, open questions and initial risk assessment. |
| Work-package approval | Defined repair scope, manuals, resources, inspection points and acceptance criteria. |
| Inspection before closure | Recorded measurements, photographs, parts fitted and independent checks where specified. |
| Static system checks | Appropriate cleanliness, leak, electrical, control and protection checks, with results recorded. |
| Readiness to start | Approved engine-specific procedure, working supporting systems, assigned personnel and emergency arrangements. |
| Controlled machinery trials | Logged pressures, temperatures, speeds, loads, leaks and observations against agreed limits. |
| Harbour and sea trials | Agreed trial programme, owner and any required authority approvals, recorded performance and defect list. |
| Handover | Closed or formally accepted defects, updated drawings, operating instructions, maintenance plan and crew familiarisation. |

A successful engine start alone does not establish readiness for sea. Confirm the complete propulsion installation, electrical supply, controls and emergency systems through the agreed verification stages.

## Machinery logbook and project records

Keep a machinery logbook recording operating hours, starts and stops, loads, pressures, temperatures, fluid additions, alarms, defects and actions. During trials, use a consistent recording interval set by the trial plan and retain instrument identification and calibration information.

Keep a separate work record for inspections and repairs, including dates, personnel, measurements, photographs, parts, specialist reports and sign-offs. Link both records to equipment identifiers so that later maintainers can trace the evidence.

## Routine maintenance and preservation

Create calendar-based and running-hour-based maintenance schedules for all relevant machinery and safety systems. Include lubrication, fluid checks, filters, exercising equipment, inspections, calibration and specialist service where required.

For the Deltic engines, use [Chapter 14: Servicing Schedules]({{< relref "/docs/T18_37K/T1837K.md" >}}#chapter-14) as the historical starting point: daily inspections, 50-, 250- and 500-hour tasks, and annual instrument checks. It describes inspection tolerances as non-cumulative. Transfer applicable tasks into a dated maintenance register with the source page, last completion, next due point and responsible person. Keep installation-component servicing and long-term preservation requirements alongside the engine schedule; the running-hour schedule alone does not establish readiness after lay-up.

Define preservation routines between work sessions and during extended lay-up, using the applicable manuals. Assign responsibility for checks and record completion. Update the maintenance plan after commissioning to reflect the restored configuration and intended operating pattern.
